Tenmile is a rural community in the wooded hills west of Winston, along Highway 42 — the old Coos Bay Wagon Road route — where the Umpqua Valley gives way to timber country on the way to Camas Valley and the coast. It takes its name from Tenmile Creek, and it is a landscape of forested slopes, small ranches, and homes tucked among the Douglas-fir. That heavier tree cover and its position closer to the coastal weather define what a Tenmile roof is up against, which is what this page covers.

What Tenmile weather does to a roof

West of Winston the country climbs into hills that catch more of the Pacific's moisture, so Tenmile tends to run wetter than the valley floor around Roseburg. Coastal systems roll up the Highway 42 corridor, fog settles into the creek draws, and the dense conifer canopy keeps north-facing roof planes damp for days at a stretch — close to ideal for the moss and lichen that lift shingle edges and wick water under the course above. Summers still turn hot and dry, baking the exposed slopes, so Tenmile roofs get worked from both ends of the cycle.

All that timber means gutters and valleys pack with fir needles and leaves every fall, and a coastal windstorm can drop limbs on roofs in the hills. Overflowing gutters, packed valleys, and branch strikes are the usual openings for a leak out here.

Signs a Tenmile roof is ready for replacement

  • Heavy moss and lichen on shaded, timbered slopes — nearly universal on the wet Highway 42 corridor.
  • Granule loss and thin spots on the sun-exposed south and west faces.
  • Curling, cupping, or brittle shingles past their prime.
  • Debris-packed valleys and overflowing gutters under the fir canopy.
  • Storm-dropped limbs or lifted shingles after a coastal windstorm.
  • Recurring leaks, or a roof past 20 years — usually the point where replacing beats patching.

Not every Tenmile roof needs replacing yet. If a roof repair is the honest answer, that is what we will tell you — and the Oregon roof-lifespan guide helps you judge where yours stands.

Tenmile permits and the no-overlay rule

Douglas County does not allow shingle-over-shingle overlays anywhere in the county, Tenmile included. Every reroof here is a full tear-off, and jobs over 100 square feet generally require a permit with kick-out flashing at sidewalls. That costs a little more than a lay-over would in some other state — and it is genuinely better, because your deck actually gets inspected and any rot gets fixed instead of buried under a second layer. We handle the paperwork and inspections; our Douglas County roofing permit guide walks through the details if you like to know what is happening.
