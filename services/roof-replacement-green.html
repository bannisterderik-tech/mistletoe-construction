Green is one of those places locals know without it showing up big on a map — the unincorporated community strung along Highway 99 and Lookingglass Road between Roseburg and Winston, out past the Douglas County Fairgrounds. It's mostly settled, suburban neighborhoods on the valley flats, and a lot of those homes went up between the 1960s and the 1990s. That timing matters: a great many Green-area roofs are now on their original layer or a first replacement that's reaching the end of its life. We're about 15–20 minutes south in Riddle and work the Green District right alongside Roseburg and Winston.

Umpqua Valley weather on the Green flats

Sitting on the open valley floor between two river drainages, Green gets the full Douglas County pattern: roughly 30-plus inches of rain from fall through spring, then hot, dry summers with 90°F-plus afternoons and weeks without rain. That wet-then-baked cycle is hard on asphalt — it soaks and swells the shingles all winter, then cooks and embrittles them all summer, which is why so many valley-floor roofs lose granules and start curling right around the 20-year mark.

The flats get decent sun, so Green roofs aren't as moss-plagued as the deep-shade canyon towns — but north-facing planes and tree-shaded yards still grow it, and the neighborhood oaks and firs keep gutters full every fall. Good drainage and sun-tolerant, algae-resistant shingles are the right recipe here.

Signs a Green-area roof is ready

  • Original roof from the '70s, '80s, or early '90s — do the math; many are simply out of years.
  • Granules in the gutters and shiny, thinning south slopes.
  • Curling, cupping, or cracked shingles from the heat cycle.
  • Moss on the shaded planes and overflowing gutters in fall.
  • Recurring leaks — the point where patching stops paying off.

If your roof is only part-way there, a repair and a maintenance plan can stretch it — our roof-lifespan guide helps you gauge where a '90s-built roof really stands.

Our Green District replacement process

  1. Free inspection and written scope — shingle line, underlayment, flashing, and warranty in writing, or a fast ballpark from the Instant Roof Estimate.
  2. Permit and prep — Douglas County permit pulled, property protected.
  3. Tear-off and rebuild — full tear-off, deck repair, synthetic underlayment, ice-and-water shield in the valleys, new flashing, and Owens Corning architectural shingles.
  4. Cleanup and walkthrough — magnetic nail sweep, haul-away, photos, and a final walk.

Permits and the no-overlay rule

Green-area reroofs follow Douglas County rules: a permit for full replacements, no overlays, and kick-out flashing at sidewalls. We handle it. On older homes that may already carry one layer, the required tear-off is the moment to inspect and repair decades-old decking instead of burying it. See our permit guide.

Green roof replacement FAQs

How much does a new roof cost in Green?

Most asphalt reroofs run roughly $10,000–$25,000 with a full tear-off; metal runs 2–3× that. Our Douglas County cost guide covers the drivers; a free written estimate gives your real number.

My house is from the 1980s and still has its first roof — is it time?

Very likely. A 1980s asphalt roof is well past typical service life in this climate. We'll inspect it free and tell you honestly whether it's a replacement or has a little life left.

Is Green covered even though it's unincorporated?

Yes — we serve the whole Green District. Call or text (541) 670-5005.

Do you pull the permit?

Yes — the Douglas County permit and inspections are part of every replacement.
