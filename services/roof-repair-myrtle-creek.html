What Myrtle Creek roofs are up against

Myrtle Creek straddles the South Umpqua where the river bends through town, and the low-lying, tree-lined riverside neighborhoods hold morning damp long after the sun is up. The area sees on the order of 36 inches of rain a year, most of it in the dark, wind-driven months between November and March. Roofs here rarely get a chance to fully dry between storms, and that steady moisture is what wears shingles out early.

The town's mix of housing matters too. Myrtle Creek has a good stock of mid-century and older homes around the historic Mill Site and downtown, many with aging flashing and a layer or two of tired shingles, alongside newer builds up the hills toward Tri City. Older roofs leak at the details — flashing, valleys, boots — while the exposed hillside roofs take the brunt of the wind.

The repairs we do most in Myrtle Creek

  • Flashing failures on older homes. The downtown and Mill Site neighborhoods have plenty of homes with reused or caulk-patched flashing that gives up in a hard rain. We replace it with proper step and kick-out flashing.
  • Riverside moss & damp-plane leaks. Shaded lots near the South Umpqua grow moss quickly on north slopes; we repair the damage and can remove the moss for good.
  • Hillside wind damage. Homes up toward Tri City and the ridges above town lose shingles to winter gusts channeled up the valley.
  • Gutter-driven leaks. Clogged gutters back water under the eaves and rot fascia — a common, cheap fix we catch early.

Permits and an honest recommendation

Small repairs typically need no permit in Douglas County, while a full reroof does — and because overlays are not allowed here, replacement means a complete tear-off. On an older Myrtle Creek home that tear-off is worth it: it exposes any soft decking hiding under two decades of shingles.
