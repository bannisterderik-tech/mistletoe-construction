Decision Guide

Roof repair or replace? How to decide.

The most expensive roofing mistake in the Umpqua Valley isn't replacing too soon — it's pouring money into patches on a roof that's already done. Here's the honest framework a Douglas County crew uses to make the call.

By Mistletoe Construction Oregon CCB #255729 Updated August 2026 8 min read

⚡ Quick answer

Repair your roof when the damage is localized — one leak, a few missing shingles, one failed flashing — on a roof under about 15 years old. Replace it when problems are widespread (multiple leaks, granule loss across the whole roof, curling shingles) or the roof is past 20. The simplest test is the 30% rule: if a repair would cost more than roughly a third of a full replacement, or fixes only a fraction of a roof that's failing everywhere, replacement is the smarter money in western Oregon's wet climate.

Every roof we look at in Douglas County lands in one of three buckets: clearly a repair, clearly a replacement, or a genuine judgment call. Most homeowners can sort their own roof into the first two before we ever climb a ladder. This guide gives you the same tests we use so you can see it coming — and so you know we're being straight with you when we tell you.

When a repair is the right answer

Repair is usually correct when the damage is contained and the roof is otherwise healthy. Classic repair situations:

  • One leak with an obvious source — a cracked pipe boot, a lifted shingle, tar-patched flashing at a single chimney.
  • A handful of shingles blown off after a windstorm rolling in off the Coast Range, on a roof that's otherwise flat and granule-covered.
  • Storm or tree damage in one area — a limb from an ice storm around Glide or Idleyld Park took out a section, but the rest is sound.
  • Flashing failure at a valley, skylight, or sidewall on a mid-life roof.

If your roof is under 15 years old and you can point to the problem, a targeted repair is almost always the honest, cheaper answer. Don't let anyone talk you into a tear-off over a $400 pipe boot.

When replacement is the right answer

Replacement wins when the whole roof surface is at end of life, not just one spot:

  • More than one leak, in different areas — the underlayment and flashing are giving out everywhere.
  • Granule loss across the field, with shiny asphalt showing and cupfuls in the gutters each year.
  • Curling, cupping, or brittle shingles that crack when handled.
  • Age past 20 — see our Oregon roof lifespan guide for why local roofs rarely hit 30.
  • Sagging deck lines or damp attic sheathing — moisture is already in the structure.
  • Established moss that returns every winter after cleaning, on an aging roof.

If you're nodding at three or more of those, patching is throwing good money after bad. Our ten signs you need a new roof guide goes deeper on each.

Three rules that make the call clear

RuleHow to use it
The age ruleUnder 15 and localized damage → lean repair. Past 20 with any widespread signs → lean replace. 15–20 is the judgment zone where an inspection decides it.
The 30% ruleIf a repair would cost more than roughly 30% of a full replacement, replacement usually delivers far more value per dollar — you're buying 25 years instead of a season or two.
The one-third ruleIf the damaged or worn area covers more than about a third of the roof, spot-repairing it rarely makes sense — the rest is close behind.

The mismatched-shingle trap

Even a clean repair on an older roof leaves a patch of new shingles that never quite matches the sun-faded originals. On a roof you plan to sell soon, or one already showing its age, that cosmetic mismatch is one more reason a mid-life "repair" sometimes tips toward replacement.

Run the actual numbers

Let's make the 30% rule concrete. Say a full replacement on your Roseburg-area home would run around $16,000. A single flashing-and-boot repair at $900 is an easy yes — that's under 6%, and it buys years. But if you're staring at a $5,000 repair (multiple areas, some deck work) on a 21-year-old roof, that's over 30% of a replacement spent on a roof that could still fail elsewhere next winter. In that case the $16,000 that ends the problem for 25 years is the better value — and right now it comes with $500 off plus free whole-home gutters on qualifying roofs ($20k+). Don't forget storm damage and insurance

If the damage came from a wind or ice storm — common on the ridgelines around Canyonville, Days Creek, and the Cascade foothills — your homeowner's policy may cover a repair or even a replacement. That can change the math entirely. Before you pay out of pocket, read our Oregon storm-damage insurance guide. We document damage with photos either way, which helps whether you file or not.

Can I just wait a year?

Sometimes, honestly, yes — a sound roof with one small issue can be repaired and monitored. But "waiting" on a roof that's actively leaking in an Umpqua Valley winter isn't saving money; it's letting water into the decking and insulation, which turns a roofing job into a roofing-plus-carpentry job. Our Home Care Membership exists for exactly this in-between: annual inspections and moss checks that catch problems while they're still cheap repairs, and stretch an aging roof to its last honest year.

Quick answers

What's the fastest way to decide?

Age plus spread. Young roof, one problem area → repair. Old roof, problems in several places → replace. The middle cases need eyes on the roof.

Is repairing an old roof ever worth it?

Only to buy a planned, short window — say you're selling next spring or saving up. As a long-term fix on a 20-plus roof, repeated repairs cost more than replacing.

Will a repair void my remaining warranty?

A proper repair by a licensed contractor won't. Sloppy DIY patches or reused flashing can. See our roofing warranty guide.
