Roof Replacement

Roof replace vs. recover (overlay).

"Can't you just put the new roof over the old one?" It's a fair question, and in a drier state the answer might be yes. In Douglas County — with our rain, our moss, and our building code — a full tear-off is almost always the right and only call. Here's why.

⚡ Quick answer

A replacement is a full tear-off to the deck; a recover (overlay) leaves the old roof on and installs new material over it. In Douglas County you generally can't overlay asphalt shingles — state code doesn't allow a second shingle layer, so every asphalt reroof here is a tear-off. Even where an overlay is technically permitted, our wet climate makes it a poor trade: it hides the deck, traps moisture, can void your warranty, and shortens roof life. The tear-off is modest money that protects the whole job. Replacement wins on nearly every Umpqua Valley home.

Every so often a homeowner in Roseburg or Sutherlin asks whether we can save them money by "recovering" the roof instead of replacing it — laying new shingles right over the tired old ones. It's a reasonable instinct. Nobody wants to pay for demolition. But roofing decisions that make sense in a dry climate can be genuinely risky here, and this is one of them. Let's walk through the real difference.

Replace vs. recover, defined

A full replacement strips the roof down to the bare wood deck. We inspect every square foot of sheathing, replace anything soft or rotted, then rebuild: synthetic underlayment, ice-and-water shield in the valleys and at penetrations, fresh flashing, and new shingles or metal. Our what a roof replacement includes guide details each layer.

A recover or overlay skips all of that. The old roofing stays, and new material goes over the top. You save the tear-off labor and dump fees — and you never find out what's happening to the deck underneath. In our climate, that's exactly the wrong thing not to know.

What Douglas County code says

For asphalt shingles, the decision is usually made for you. Douglas County follows the Oregon building code, which does not allow a new layer of asphalt shingles over an existing one — a reroof means a tear-off. Code also prohibits recovering over roofing that's water-soaked, buckled, or deteriorated, and that describes a lot of the aging roofs we see across the Umpqua Valley. We pull the permit and build to code on every job; our Douglas County roofing permit guide covers the details, and the local rule also shows up on our Roseburg roof replacement page.

The deck is the whole argument

Here's the heart of it. The wood deck under your shingles is where western Oregon does its quiet damage. Thirty-plus inches of annual rain, moss holding moisture against shaded north slopes, and old leaks that wicked into the plywood leave the deck soft, delaminated, or rotting in spots invisible from on top. An overlay buries that. The rot doesn't stop — it keeps spreading under a brand-new roof you expected to forget about for two decades. A tear-off ends the guessing: we see the deck, fix what's bad, and build on something we've actually verified.

The false economy of an overlay

An overlay saves you the tear-off cost today. But it also adds weight to the structure, hides deck rot that keeps growing, telegraphs the bumps of the old roof through the new one, can void your shingle warranty, and typically gives you a shorter roof life. The "savings" often evaporate the first time water finds the deck you never inspected.

The cost difference, honestly

The tear-off and disposal is a genuine line item, but usually a modest share of a full reroof. Weigh it against what it buys: an inspected, sound deck, fresh underlayment and ice-and-water shield, correct flashing, a full manufacturer warranty, and a roof that reaches its real lifespan instead of a shortened one. For the numbers, our Oregon roof replacement cost guide and repair vs. full replacement cost guide lay it out.

When it's replace vs. repair (not overlay)

The real decision most homeowners face isn't overlay vs. replace — it's repair vs. replace. One problem area on an otherwise healthy roof under 15 years old is usually a targeted repair. Several signs of failure on a roof past 20 point to replacement. Our repair-or-replace decision guide walks the math, and the signs you need a new roof help you read yours.

Quick answers

Is a recover always cheaper than a replacement?

Up front, a little — you skip tear-off and disposal. Over the roof's life it often costs more, because you didn't fix the deck and the roof doesn't last as long.

Can I overlay to sell my house faster?

We'd advise against it. A layered roof and hidden deck issues can surface in a buyer's inspection, and a proper tear-off with a transferable warranty is a stronger selling point.

What if my roof is only a few years old but leaking?

Then it's probably a repair, not a reroof at all. We'll find the source and fix it — see our how to find a roof leak guide.
