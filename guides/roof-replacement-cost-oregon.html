2026 Oregon price ranges

For a typical single-family home, here is what we see across the state this year:

  • Architectural asphalt replacement: most jobs land between $10,000 and $25,000, tear-off included. A small 15–18 square ranch can dip below $10,000; a 30+ square home with steep, complicated planes can pass $25,000.
  • Per square (100 sq ft): roughly $475–$775 per square installed for architectural asphalt, covering tear-off, underlayment, flashing, and shingles. The low end reflects walkable roofs; the high end reflects steep, hand-nailed, harness-required work.
  • Metal roofing: plan on about 1.9× the asphalt number for standing-seam. It lasts 40–70 years and sheds Oregon's moss and needle litter far better, so the lifetime math is closer than the sticker suggests — we break it down in our metal roof cost guide and metal vs. asphalt comparison.
  • Tile and specialty: tile and designer profiles can run 2× or more over asphalt once you add framing and labor.

If a bid lands dramatically below these ranges, pause. Someone is trimming something you can't see yet — underlayment, flashing, permit, or insurance — and the savings tend to reappear in year six.

Why Oregon's climate changes the math

Oregon isn't one climate, and that shows up in roofing cost. West of the Cascades — Portland, Salem, Eugene, and down through the Umpqua Valley where we work — homes take 30–45+ inches of rain a year, most of it between October and May, followed by hot, bone-dry summers. That wet-dry whiplash ages asphalt faster than the national average, so wet-side homeowners lean toward better underlayment, ice-and-water shield in the valleys, and algae-resistant architectural shingles. Those choices cost a little more up front and pay for themselves in years of extra roof life.

East of the Cascades — Bend, Redmond, Klamath Falls — the story flips to snow load, freeze-thaw, and intense UV, which puts a premium on ventilation and ice protection instead. Either way, the biggest cost driver isn't the region; it's your specific roof.

What actually moves your number

FactorEffect on price
Roof sizeThe biggest lever. Roofing is sold per square, so a 28-square roof simply costs more than an 18-square one across materials, labor, and disposal.
PitchA 3/12–4/12 roof might price near $475/square; a steep 7/12–8/12 needing harnesses and staging climbs toward $775/square. 9/12 and up is custom-quoted.
Tear-off layersTwo or three old layers doubles or triples tear-off labor and dump fees. Wet-side Oregon almost always requires a full tear-off, no overlays.
Deck repairRot and delaminated sheathing get replaced by the sheet once the roof is open. Honest bids state a per-sheet price up front.
ComplexityValleys, dormers, skylights, and chimneys mean cutting and flashing. A cut-up roof costs more per square than a simple gable of the same size.
Material tier3-tab vs. architectural vs. metal vs. tile. Architectural is the wet-Oregon sweet spot — better wind ratings and roughly 10 more years than 3-tab.

The tear-off rule that raises (and protects) your cost

In much of the country, roofers nail a second layer over the first and skip tear-off. On the wet side of Oregon that's usually off the table — Douglas County and many neighboring jurisdictions do not allow shingle-over-shingle overlays, and reroofs over 100 square feet generally require a permit. That makes a local job cost more than a national overlay average, and it's worth it: overlays trap heat, hide rot, add dead weight, and turn the next reroof into a double tear-off. A full tear-off means your deck actually gets inspected and repaired. Our Douglas County permit guide covers the specifics.

How to compare Oregon roofing bids

Three bids on the same roof can differ by thousands and all be "fair" — they're pricing different jobs. Before you compare totals, make every bidder put these in writing:

  1. Exact scope. Full tear-off? How many layers assumed? Permit pulled by the contractor?
  2. The shingle, by name. Manufacturer and product line, not "30-year architectural."
  3. Underlayment. Synthetic or felt, plus ice-and-water membrane in valleys and at penetrations. Our ice-and-water shield explainer shows why this matters here.
  4. Flashing plan. New step flashing, kick-outs, and pipe boots — reused flashing is a leading cause of early leaks.
  5. Deck repair pricing. A stated per-sheet price agreed before tear-off.
  6. License verification. Look up every CCB number at search.ccb.state.or.us — two minutes for license, bond, insurance, and complaints. Our CCB verification guide walks through it. (We're CCB #255729 — check us, please.)
