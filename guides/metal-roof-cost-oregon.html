Metal roof cost in Oregon: standing-seam & corrugated, priced honestly.

Metal costs more up front than asphalt — but in a climate that grows moss on north-facing shingles and drops fir needles all winter, it can be the last roof you ever buy. Here are real 2026 ranges, tied to size, pitch, and panel type.

Quick answer

In 2026, a metal roof in Oregon typically costs about 1.9× an equivalent architectural asphalt roof. Since asphalt runs roughly $475–$775 per square by pitch, standing-seam metal lands near $900–$1,475 per square installed, and most whole-home metal roofs fall between about $20,000 and $45,000. Corrugated/through-fastened panels cost less than standing-seam. Metal lasts 40–70 years versus roughly 20–25 for asphalt here, so the lifetime cost is closer than the sticker suggests. 2026 metal roof price ranges

  • Standing-seam: roughly $900–$1,475 per square installed — about 1.9× architectural asphalt — with concealed fasteners and clean, screwless lines. Most whole-home standing-seam roofs run $20,000–$45,000.
  • Corrugated / through-fastened: less than standing-seam per square, because exposed-fastener panels install faster. A budget-conscious way into metal, popular on shops, barns, and simpler home rooflines. Our standing-seam vs. corrugated guide compares them in depth.
  • By comparison, asphalt: about $475–$775 per square, or $10,000–$25,000 for most homes — see the full Oregon roof replacement cost guide.

Metal's price spread is wide because panel gauge, coating, trim details, and roof complexity all matter more than they do with shingles. A simple gable is far cheaper to panel than a roof full of valleys, dormers, and dead-end walls.

How pitch and size drive the price

Metal follows the same two big levers as asphalt: square footage and pitch. A walkable 3/12–4/12 roof sits at the low end of the per-square range; a steep 7/12–8/12 roof that demands staging, harnesses, and careful panel handling climbs toward the high end. Anything 9/12 or steeper we price on site. Bigger roofs cost more in total but sometimes a little less per square, since setup and delivery spread across more area.

Why metal can pay for itself in Oregon

The wet side of Oregon is hard on asphalt. Shaded, north-facing planes grow moss that lifts shingle edges and holds water; fir and oak litter piles in valleys; and the wet-winter, hot-summer cycle bakes then soaks the granules until they wash into the gutters. Metal sidesteps most of that. It sheds debris and water, resists the moss that shortens shingle life, and typically lasts 40–70 years versus roughly 20–25 for asphalt locally.

Run the per-year math: a $30,000 metal roof over 50 years is $600/year; a $16,000 asphalt roof replaced every 22 years is roughly $730/year before counting moss treatments and repairs. Metal also reflects summer heat and pairs well with solar. Our metal vs. asphalt comparison lays out the full trade-off, and Class A fire ratings make it appealing in the wildfire-aware parts of the county.

Comparing metal bids fairly

Metal bids vary even more than asphalt, so get these in writing before you compare totals:

  1. Panel system and gauge. Standing-seam vs. through-fastened, and the steel gauge (lower number = thicker).
  2. Coating. The paint system drives fade and chalk resistance over decades.
  3. Underlayment. A high-temp synthetic or ice-and-water membrane belongs under metal — see our ice-and-water shield explainer.
  4. Trim and flashing. Ridge, eave, valley, and sidewall details are where cheap metal jobs leak.
  5. CCB license. Verify every bidder at search.ccb.state.or.us — our CCB guide shows how. We're CCB #255729.

Quick answers

What does a metal roof cost in Oregon?

About 1.9× asphalt — roughly $900–$1,475 per square for standing-seam, or $20,000–$45,000 for most homes. Corrugated costs less.

Standing-seam or corrugated?

Corrugated is cheaper up front; standing-seam hides its fasteners, lasts longer, and handles heavy rain better. Both beat asphalt on lifespan.

Is metal worth it here?

Often — it sheds moss and debris, lasts 40–70 years, and its cost per year can beat asphalt in our wet climate.
