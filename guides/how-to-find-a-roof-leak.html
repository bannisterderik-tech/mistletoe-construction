Roof Leaks

How to find a roof leak.

The ceiling stain lies about where the water gets in. Here's the step-by-step our Riddle-based crew uses to trace an Umpqua Valley leak to its real source — plus the local spots that fail first, and when to stop and call instead of climbing a slick roof.

⚡ Quick answer

Find a roof leak by working from the inside out. On a rainy day, go into the attic and follow the water stains uphill along the rafters and sheathing — water enters higher than it drips, so mark the highest wet point. Then check the roof directly above it for the usual suspects: pipe boots, chimney and wall flashing, valleys, and lifted or missing shingles. The leak is almost never directly over the ceiling stain. If it's steep, mossy, or you can't trace it, stop and call — a wrong patch just hides the real source.

We track down leaks all over Douglas County, and the pattern is always the same: the homeowner points at the ceiling stain, and the actual leak turns out to be several feet away and uphill. Western Oregon rain is relentless and patient — it finds a gap, runs along the wood, and picks a spot to drip that has nothing to do with where it got in. So finding a leak isn't about looking straight up. It's detective work, and it starts inside.

Step 1: Start in the attic

The attic is your best evidence room, and the safest place to work. On a rainy day (or right after), take a flashlight up and look at the underside of the roof deck. You're hunting for dark water staining on the sheathing, damp or discolored insulation, drips on the rafters, or a musty smell. Follow any wet trail uphill to its highest point — that's closest to where water is actually entering. Mark it, and measure roughly where it sits relative to a vent, chimney, or wall you can find from outside.

Step 2: Check the usual suspects above it

Once you know the rough area, the source is usually one of a short list of culprits — the same ones that fail first on Umpqua Valley homes:

  • Pipe boots. The rubber collar around plumbing vents cracks and dries out. This is the single most common leak we find, period.
  • Chimney & sidewall flashing. The metal sealing roof-to-wall joints rusts, lifts, or was "fixed" with tar that's since cracked. Our flashing guide shows what good detail looks like.
  • Valleys. Where two roof planes meet, they carry the most water — and leak when the metal or shingle weave wears.
  • Missing or lifted shingles. Common after a wind event off the Coast Range, or where moss has pried edges up.
  • Clogged gutters. Under Douglas-fir and oak canopy, gutters pack with needles and back water up under the shingle edge. See our gutter guide for Oregon rain.

Step 3: The water test (for stubborn leaks)

If a leak only shows up sometimes — in wind-driven rain, or after days of soaking — reproducing it is the way to pin it down. With one person watching the attic stain and another running a garden hose on the roof, you wet one section at a time, starting low and working uphill, holding each area for several minutes. When the drip appears inside, you've found it. This controlled test is exactly how we catch the intermittent leaks our climate loves to hide, and it beats guessing every time.

The moss connection

Many Douglas County leaks trace back to moss. On shaded north slopes it lifts shingle edges and holds water against the deck, feeding leaks that a dry-climate roof would never get. If you're finding leaks and moss together, treating the moss is part of the fix — see our moss prevention guide and moss removal service.

When to stop and call

Do the attic detective work — it's safe and genuinely useful. But don't walk the roof. Western Oregon roofs are steep, and rain and moss make them slick enough to put people in the hospital. And a DIY patch in the wrong spot usually caulks over the symptom while water keeps soaking the deck behind it, turning a $300 fix into a rotted-sheathing job. Quick answers

Where do most roof leaks actually start?

Pipe boots and flashing, by a wide margin. Both are penetrations or joints where the roof surface is interrupted — the weak points water finds first.

Can I find a leak when it's not raining?

Yes, with the garden-hose water test, or by reading dried water stains and rust patterns in the attic. But a live rain is the easiest time to trace an active drip.

My ceiling is stained but there's no drip — is it still a roof leak?

Possibly, or it could be condensation or a plumbing issue. Attic inspection tells the difference, and it's one thing we check on every leak call.
