Roof repair vs full replacement: the honest cost decision.

Patch it or replace it? It's the most expensive fork in homeownership, and the wrong call costs thousands either way. Here's the repair-or-replace framework we use on real Douglas County roofs — including when we tell people not to buy the bigger job.

By Mistletoe Construction Oregon CCB #255729 Updated August 2026 8 min read

⚡ Quick answer

Repair is the smart money when the damage is isolated and the rest of the roof is healthy — most single-issue repairs in Douglas County run a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars. Replace when problems are becoming a pattern, the roof is past 20-25 years, or moss and deck rot are widespread, since a full asphalt replacement ($10,000-$25,000 here) stops you paying for the same leak twice. The deciding factor is remaining roof life, not the price of today's patch.

Every roofing company faces a temptation on this question: replacement is the bigger ticket, so it's tempting to steer everyone toward it. We don't run that way — we sell plenty of repairs and we're glad to. The goal here is to give you the same reasoning we'd use standing on your roof in Roseburg or Canyonville, so you can push back on anyone who tries to oversell you.

The break-even math nobody explains

Here's the frame that actually settles it. Don't compare the repair price to the replacement price — compare cost per remaining year of roof life.

  • A $1,200 repair on a 10-year-old roof with 12+ good years left? That's roughly $100/year of protection. Excellent value — take it.
  • The same $1,200 repair on a 24-year-old roof that's shedding granules everywhere? You might buy 18 months before the next leak somewhere else. That's $800/year, and you'll likely be replacing anyway. Now the repair is throwing good money after bad.

The trap is that both roofs look like they just need "one fix." The difference is the condition of everything you're not looking at. That's what an honest inspection is for — see our inspection checklist to know what to examine before you decide.

6 signs it's time to replace, not repair

  1. Multiple leaks in different areas. One leak is a repair. Three leaks in three spots means the system is failing, not a single component.
  2. Widespread granule loss. Bald patches and granules piling in the gutters mean the shingle mat is exposed and aging fast — see our guide on how long a roof lasts in Oregon.
  3. Curling, cupping, or brittle shingles across the roof. When shingles crack instead of flex, a repair just breaks the neighbors.
  4. Sagging deck lines. A dip or wave in the roof plane signals rotted sheathing or structural moisture — that's beyond patching.
  5. Heavy moss with soft decking underneath. In the Umpqua Valley, unmanaged moss lifts shingles and holds water against the deck until it rots. Spongy sheathing means replacement.
  6. Age past 20-25 years. An asphalt roof at the end of its rated life will keep finding new places to fail. At that point replacement is cheaper than a repair-every-winter subscription.

When we tell you to repair, not replace

A single wind-lifted section, a failed pipe boot, one bad valley, or storm damage on an otherwise solid roof — those are repairs, full stop. We'll fix it, document it, and send you on your way. If a contractor insists a young, healthy roof needs full replacement over one leak, get a second opinion. And if storm damage is involved, our insurance claims guide helps you handle it right.

The Douglas County wrinkle: no overlays

One local rule shapes this decision: Douglas County doesn't allow shingle-over-shingle overlays. When you do replace, it's a full tear-off to the deck, and reroofs over 100 square feet generally need a permit (details in our permits guide). That's actually good news for the repair-vs-replace call: a proper tear-off means hidden deck rot gets found and fixed rather than buried under a second layer, so your eventual replacement is a genuine reset — not a problem postponed.

What a good repair bid actually includes

If you land on repair, the bid should be as specific as a replacement bid — vague "we'll patch the leak" pricing is how small problems become recurring ones. A solid Douglas County repair scope names the actual failure (a lifted section, a cracked pipe boot, a failed valley, worn flashing at a sidewall), states whether any decking needs replacing and at what per-sheet price, and specifies matching shingle and new flashing rather than reused metal. Reused flashing is one of the most common causes of a "repaired" leak coming back a season later.

It should also come from a CCB-licensed roofer — verify our Oregon CCB #255729 at the state site in about two minutes, exactly as our contractor verification guide describes. Watch for the storm-chaser pattern too: out-of-area crews who appear after a big blow, push a fast full replacement on a roof that only needs a repair, and are gone before the callback. Quick answers

Is it cheaper to repair or replace?

Repair is cheaper up front — a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars versus $10,000–$25,000 to replace. It's the smart money when the roof is otherwise healthy; replacement wins once repairs become a pattern.

When should I replace instead?

Widespread granule loss, curling shingles, multiple leaks, sagging deck, heavy moss with soft decking, or age past 20–25 years all point to replacement.

Can I patch over the old roof here?

You can repair sections, but Douglas County requires a full tear-off for replacement — no overlays — and reroofs over 100 sq ft generally need a permit.

Will a repair void my warranty?

Not when done by a licensed contractor with documentation. Verify the CCB number and keep your paperwork.
