Asphalt vs metal roof lifespan in western Oregon: the real numbers.

National lifespan charts assume dry heat and no moss. The Umpqua Valley is neither. Here's how long asphalt and metal roofs actually last in Douglas County's rain, what shortens each, and the cost-per-year math that settles the decision.

⚡ Quick answer

In western Oregon, architectural asphalt shingles typically last about 20-25 years (less if moss goes unmanaged), while a quality metal roof lasts about 40-70 years. Our wet, mossy climate is harder on asphalt than the national averages assume, and much kinder to metal, because moss can't grip metal's smooth surface. On a cost-per-year basis the two land closer than the sticker prices suggest — metal wins for long-term owners, asphalt for tighter budgets or shorter horizons.

"How long will it last?" is the first question every homeowner asks, and it's the one where generic online numbers mislead people the most. Those charts are built for Phoenix and Denver. Down here in Douglas County — over 30 inches of rain a year, mild wet winters, and moss growing like a crop — roofs age on a different clock. We install both materials, so here's the honest local picture.

Notice the asphalt figure is lower than the "30-year shingle" label on the bundle. That label is a lab rating, not a Umpqua Valley rating. Our how long a roof lasts in Oregon guide digs into why real-world local life runs shorter than the packaging promises.

Why moss widens the gap here

This is the local factor that flips the usual math. In dry climates, asphalt and metal lifespans are closer together, so metal's premium is harder to justify. In the Umpqua Valley, moss is the number one killer of asphalt roofs — its rhizoids grip the granular shingle surface, lift the edges, and wick water underneath until the mat degrades and the deck suffers. Metal's smooth painted surface gives moss nothing to hold, and fir needles slide off instead of composting in the valleys. So our climate simultaneously shortens asphalt life and preserves metal life, widening the gap. Managing moss with our prevention routine or a professional treatment is how you keep asphalt at the top of its range.

The cost-per-year math

Lifespan only matters next to price. Here's the frame that settles most decisions (all figures estimates — your written estimate is the real math):

  • Asphalt: say $16,000 lasting ~22 years with maintenance → roughly $730/year, plus moss treatments along the way.
  • Metal: say $38,000 lasting ~50 years → roughly $760/year, with minimal maintenance.

Per year of service they're nearly the same roof. The real difference is when you pay and how many times: asphalt spreads the cost across two or three replacements at future prices; metal front-loads it once. If this is your forever home, metal's one-and-done lifespan is compelling. Selling within 5-10 years? You'd pay metal's full premium and hand most of its lifespan to the next owner. Lifespan is half material, half installation

A premium shingle installed over cheap flashing and poor attic ventilation won't reach its rated life — the deck cooks and the seams fail early. A mid-tier roof installed with new flashing, proper venting, and ice-and-water shield in the valleys can outlast it. When you compare lifespan claims, compare the installer just as hard as the material.

How to get the top of the range from either

  1. Manage moss relentlessly on asphalt. The single biggest lever. Treat annually, keep valleys clear, add zinc strips.
  2. Ventilate the attic. Balanced intake and exhaust keeps the deck dry and the shingles from baking from below.
  3. Keep gutters flowing. Overflowing, clogged gutters hold water against fascia and roof edges — a slow-motion lifespan killer on both materials. Consider gutter guards.
  4. Inspect yearly. Catch a lifted shingle or failing boot before it becomes a deck-rot project. Our Home Care Membership (from $49/month) builds this in.

What we see on real Douglas County roofs

Rated lifespans are averages; the roofs we actually walk tell a more specific story. On the shaded north slopes and tree-lined lots common around the South Umpqua and along the river corridors from Winston to Canyonville, unmanaged asphalt often shows serious moss and granule loss by year 15-18 — well short of its rated life. The same shingle on a sunny, open lot in Green or on a ridge above Roseburg, treated for moss and properly ventilated, routinely reaches 22-25 years looking respectable. Elevation matters too: higher, colder spots toward Glide see more freeze-thaw cycling that works at brittle older shingles.

Two other local wrinkles shape real-world life. Wildfire smoke and ash from late-summer seasons settle into valleys and, mixed with needles and moss, hold moisture against asphalt — one more reason our roofs need the clearing routine in our smoke and ash guide. And atmospheric-river wind funneling down the Umpqua corridors tests shingle seals for hours at a time; a properly nailed architectural roof shrugs it off, but an aging or poorly installed one starts losing tabs, which is often the beginning of the end. Metal's interlocked panels handle those same valley windstorms with room to spare.

Metal, by contrast, ages so slowly that what usually "dates" it is the finish, not the function. We've seen panel roofs from decades back still keeping water out long after the paint softened. The failure mode is almost never the metal itself — it's a neglected fastener or a sealant at a penetration that should have been checked. That's the practical difference for a homeowner: an asphalt roof's clock is set by moisture and moss you have to actively fight; a metal roof's clock is set by a handful of details you occasionally inspect. From our Riddle base we service both across the county, and the pattern holds street after street.

Quick answers

How long does asphalt last here?

About 20–25 years in the Umpqua Valley, less if moss is ignored. That's shorter than sunny-climate averages because moisture and moss are hard on shingles.

How long does metal last?

Roughly 40–70 years. The smooth surface sheds moss and needles, so our climate is easy on metal.

Is metal's longer life worth the cost?

Cost-per-year, the two are close. Metal wins for long-term owners; quality asphalt is a genuinely good roof for tighter budgets or shorter horizons.

What shortens roof life most here?

Unmanaged moss, poor attic ventilation, cheap or reused flashing, and clogged gutters. Annual inspections and keeping the roof clear help both materials last.
