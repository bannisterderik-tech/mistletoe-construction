Storm Damage Guide

Roof warning signs after a storm.

A wind-loaded winter storm just rolled through Douglas County. Before the next one arrives, here's exactly what to check — safely, from the ground — and how to tell the difference between cosmetic and call-someone-now.

⚡ Quick answer

After an Oregon storm, check from the ground for missing, lifted, or curled shingles; shingle pieces and granule piles in the yard; bent or torn flashing; a leaning gutter; and fresh limbs or debris on the roof. Inside, look in the attic and at ceilings for new water stains or daylight. Don't climb up — a wet, wind-damaged roof is dangerous. Our winters don't do gentle. The storms that come off the Pacific and slam into the Coast Range funnel down into the Umpqua Valley as wind — and wind is what damages roofs here more than anything else. One good blow can lift shingles you'd never notice from the driveway, and by the time the ceiling stains show up in February, water's already been in the deck for weeks. This guide helps you catch it early and safely.

What actually hits roofs in the Umpqua Valley

Knowing the threat tells you what to look for:

  • Winter windstorms. The dominant risk. Gusts funneling through the valley lift shingle tabs and peel back edges, especially on older 3-tab roofs. Wind damage is often subtle — a few lifted shingles, not a hole.
  • Falling trees and limbs. Saturated ground plus wind brings down Douglas-fir and oak branches onto roofs across the county — one of the most common Douglas County claims. Impact can crack shingles and puncture the deck.
  • Ice storms. Less frequent than rain, but hard ice events load and crack shingles and gutters, and hit higher spots like Glide and the Cascade foothills harder than the valley floor.
  • Hail. Uncommon here compared to the Midwest, but it does occur and can bruise shingles, knocking granules loose.
  • Snow load. Rare in Roseburg, Winston, or Sutherlin; a real factor east toward Glide and the mountains.

The outside warning signs (check from the ground)

Grab binoculars and walk the perimeter of the house. Look for:

  • Missing, lifted, or curled shingles. Gaps in the pattern, tabs standing up, or corners peeled back. Even a few lifted shingles break the water seal.
  • Shingle pieces and granule piles in the yard, on the driveway, or at the base of downspouts. Fresh granule loss after a storm means the surface took a beating.
  • Damaged or detached flashing around chimneys, vents, and skylights — bent, lifted, or missing. Flashing is where most storm leaks start. Our flashing guide explains why.
  • Gutter trouble. A leaning, sagging, or pulled-away gutter, or one packed with granules and shingle bits. Storms and clogged gutters go together in our fir-and-oak country.
  • Debris and limbs on the roof. Anything sitting on the shingles traps water and may have already done damage on impact.
  • Displaced ridge cap along the peak — the most wind-exposed part of the roof.

⚠ Do not get on the roof

A wet, wind-damaged roof is genuinely dangerous, and walking a compromised roof causes more damage. Inspect from the ground and from inside the attic. Leave the actual roof to a roofer with fall protection — that's literally our job, and our inspection is free.

The inside warning signs (check the attic and ceilings)

Some storm damage never shows on the shingles — it shows indoors, and it's the most urgent kind because it means water is already getting in:

  • New water stains — brown rings or streaks on ceilings and upper walls that weren't there before.
  • Daylight in the attic. Go up with a flashlight on a dry day; any pinpoint of daylight through the roof boards is a breach.
  • Damp insulation or a musty smell, or dark staining on the underside of the deck.
  • Dripping or active leaking during the next rain — an emergency. See our roof-leak emergency guide for the first steps.

What to do next

  1. Mitigate. Tarp active leaks and move valuables out from under drips. Insurers expect you to prevent further damage.
  2. Document. Take dated photos of everything you can safely see, inside and out, and note the storm date. This is what gets an insurance claim approved.
  3. Get a professional inspection. We get on the roof safely, photograph the full picture, and tell you honestly whether it's a repair, a replacement, or nothing to worry about.
  4. Decide on a claim. Our guides on whether insurance covers roof replacement in Oregon and filing storm-damage claims walk through it.

Quick answers

What's the most common storm damage here?

Wind-lifted and missing shingles, plus fallen fir and oak limbs. Wind is subtle — a few lifted tabs, not always a visible hole — which is why a post-storm inspection matters.

Can I check the damage myself?

From the ground and the attic, yes — with binoculars and a flashlight. Never climb a wet, wind-damaged roof. Photograph what you see and call a licensed roofer for the close look.

How fast do I need to act?

Fast. The next rain is rarely far off here, and even minor lifted shingles admit water. Tarp active leaks, document for insurance, and get an inspection right away.

Is the damage covered by insurance?

Sudden storm damage — wind, fallen trees, hail, ice — is often covered after your deductible; age and wear are not. Dated documentation is what gets a claim approved.
