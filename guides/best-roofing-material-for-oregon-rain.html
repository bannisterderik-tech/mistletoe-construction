Materials Guide

The best roofing material for Oregon rain.

Thirty-plus inches of rain, 130-plus wet days, and moss that treats a north-facing slope like a garden. Here's how the roofing materials really stack up in the Umpqua Valley — from a crew that installs them here, in this weather.

⚡ Quick answer

For most Douglas County homes, architectural asphalt shingles with algae-resistant granules are the best value in the rain — they shed water, resist moss streaking, and cost far less than metal. For maximum lifespan and the cleanest moss-shedding on shaded roofs, standing-seam metal is the top performer at roughly 2–3× the price. The material to avoid here is cheap 3-tab asphalt, which ages fast and grows moss quickly in western Oregon's wet climate.

The Umpqua Valley is a specific kind of wet. The Coast Range squeezes the rain out of Pacific storms before they reach Roseburg, so we get long, saturating stretches November through April rather than dramatic downpours. Roofs stay damp for weeks. Douglas fir shade keeps north slopes from ever fully drying. Then summer flips the switch and bakes everything. A roofing material that thrives in Phoenix or even Portland can disappoint here — so let's rank them by how they actually handle our rain and moss.

Architectural asphalt shingles — the local default

This is what goes on the majority of Douglas County roofs, and for good reason. Architectural (also called laminate or dimensional) shingles are thicker and heavier than old 3-tab, with better wind ratings and roughly 10 more years of life. The versions we install carry algae-resistant granules — copper-infused granules that fight the black streaking our climate loves to grow.

  • Rain performance: excellent when installed with synthetic underlayment and ice-and-water shield in the valleys.
  • Moss resistance: good with AR granules and correct attic ventilation; still needs periodic cleaning on shaded slopes.
  • Lifespan here: about 18–25 years.
  • Cost: the most affordable full-quality option.

As an Owens Corning contractor we typically install their Duration line. If you're comparing shingle tiers, our architectural vs. 3-tab guide breaks down why the upgrade is worth it in this climate.

Metal roofing — the long-haul champion

If you're staying put for decades or fighting a chronically mossy roof, metal is the best rain performer we install. A smooth standing-seam metal roof sheds water almost instantly and gives moss, needles, and leaves nothing to grip — a real advantage on the shaded lots common around Glide, Winston, and the Cascade foothills.

  • Rain performance: the best — water is gone before it can pool or wick.
  • Moss resistance: excellent; the slick surface stays clean where shingles streak.
  • Lifespan here: 40–70 years.
  • Cost: roughly 2–3× architectural asphalt.

The upfront cost is the catch. We lay out the full trade-off in our metal vs. asphalt comparison and our deep dive on whether a metal roof is worth it in Oregon. There's also a style choice within metal — see standing seam vs. corrugated.

The alternatives — and why they're rarer here

MaterialHow it does in Oregon rain
3-tab asphaltThe budget shingle. Thinner, weaker wind rating, and grows moss and fails faster in our wet-dry cycle. We rarely recommend it — the small savings evaporate in lost years.
Wood shakesBeautiful, and a disaster in this climate. Cedar holds moisture, feeds moss and rot, and raises wildfire concern. Most local insurers and our wildfire-resistant roofing priorities push away from it.
Concrete / clay tileDurable and sheds rain well, but heavy — many local roofs need structural reinforcement — and the porous surface can still hold moss on shaded slopes. A niche choice here.
Synthetic / compositeImproving fast, with good rain and moss performance and lighter weight than tile. Pricier than asphalt and less proven long-term locally, but a reasonable premium option.
Low-slope membrane (TPO/torch-down)Not a pitched-roof choice — it's for flat and low-slope sections like porches and additions. Covered in our flat & low-slope guide.

The material choice that matters more than you think

Here's what the material debates usually miss: in our climate, what's under the shingles decides whether the roof leaks. Synthetic underlayment across the whole deck, ice-and-water membrane in the valleys and around every penetration, and proper flashing are the difference between a roof that survives a March atmospheric river and one that doesn't. A premium shingle over cheap felt and reused flashing is a worse roof than a mid shingle done right. Read why ice-and-water shield matters before you compare bids on material alone.

Match the material to the slope

North-facing and tree-shaded slopes are where moss wins. If most of your roof is shaded, the extra cost of metal — or at minimum AR-granule architectural shingles with a moss-prevention plan — pays off. A sun-exposed roof gives asphalt an easier life and a longer one.

So what should you actually put on your house?

Our honest default for Douglas County: architectural asphalt with algae-resistant granules, installed with synthetic underlayment and full flashing, for the best balance of cost and rain performance. Choose standing-seam metal if you'll own the home for decades, have a heavily shaded roof, or simply want to stop thinking about the roof for 50 years. Skip 3-tab and cedar in this climate. Quick answers

Best all-around material for our rain?

Architectural asphalt with AR granules for value; standing-seam metal for maximum lifespan and moss-shedding. Both beat 3-tab and cedar here.

Is metal worth 2–3× the cost?

If you're staying long-term or fighting shade and moss, often yes. If you may move within 15 years, quality asphalt usually makes more financial sense.

What's the worst material for Oregon?

Cheap 3-tab asphalt and wood shakes — both age fast, grow moss, and, for cedar, raise wildfire and insurance concerns.
