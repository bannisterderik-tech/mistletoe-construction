Roofing Basics

What is a roofing square?

It's the single most-used word on a roofing estimate — and one almost no homeowner is taught. Here's what a roofing square really is, how many your Douglas County home likely has, and how it shapes the number on your quote.

⚡ Quick answer

A roofing square equals 100 square feet of roof surface — a 10-foot by 10-foot patch. Roofers measure and price roofs in squares because shingles, underlayment, and labor all scale by the square. A roof with 2,000 square feet of surface is 20 squares. It measures the actual sloped roof, not your home's footprint — so a steep roof has more squares than the house is wide. A typical Douglas County home runs roughly 15–30 squares. When you see a per-square price on an estimate, that's the building block the whole quote is built from.

When we hand a homeowner in Roseburg or Winston their estimate and say "your roof is about 22 squares," the usual response is a polite nod and a hidden question: what does that mean? Fair enough — "square" is roofing jargon that nobody explains. Understanding it turns your estimate from a mystery into something you can actually check. So here's the whole idea, in plain English.

The definition: 100 square feet

A roofing square is simply 100 square feet of roof area — picture a patch 10 feet by 10 feet. That's it. It's a unit of measurement, like a dozen or a gallon, invented so roofers and manufacturers can talk about roof size in a consistent way. If your roof has 1,800 square feet of surface, that's 18 squares. If it's 2,600 square feet, that's 26 squares. Divide the total roof area by 100 and you have your square count.

One thing to get straight right away: a roofing square is not the footprint of your house. It's the area of the actual sloped roof surface, which is larger than the ground the house sits on — more on why below.

Why roofers use squares

The reason is practical. Shingles, underlayment, and most roofing materials are manufactured and sold by the square. A bundle of architectural shingles covers a set fraction of a square; three bundles typically make one square. Labor is estimated per square too. So when a roofer prices your job, the square is the natural building block — it keeps the material order, the labor time, and the waste calculation all lined up. It's been the industry standard for over a century, and every honest estimate you compare will be built on it. Our guide on how to read a roofing estimate shows exactly where the square count shows up on the paperwork.

Why pitch makes your roof bigger than your house

Here's the part that surprises people. Your home's footprint — the outline on the ground — is not your roof area, because the roof is sloped. The steeper the pitch, the more surface there is stretched over that same footprint. Think of a tent: a steep-sided tent uses more fabric than a flat one covering the same patch of ground. A 1,500-square-foot single-story home might have 1,800 or 2,000 square feet of actual roof surface once you account for the slope and overhangs. That's why we can't quote a roof from the tax record's house size alone — pitch and roofline complexity change the square count significantly. Our shingle guide covers how material choice interacts with all this.

Quick math example

Say your roof has two main planes, each 40 feet long and 25 feet up the slope. That's 40 × 25 = 1,000 sq ft per plane, 2,000 sq ft total, which is 20 squares. Add a waste factor for cuts at the hips, valleys, and edges — usually around 10% — and we'd order material for roughly 22 squares. That waste factor is normal and honest, not padding.

How many squares is a typical Oregon home?

For planning, most single-family homes in Douglas County land in the 15 to 30 square range. A compact ranch might be 15–18 squares; a larger two-story with dormers, a wrapped porch, and cut-up valleys can push past 30. The exact number depends on square footage, pitch, and how complex the roofline is. When you see cost figures quoted "per square," you can multiply by your rough square count to sanity-check a ballpark — then let a written estimate nail it down. How we count your squares

Measuring is straightforward: we take the length and width of each roof plane, multiply to get each plane's area, add them all up, divide by 100 for squares, and add a waste factor for the cuts at hips, valleys, and edges. We account for pitch so the slope isn't undercounted, and we use both aerial measurement tools and on-roof measuring to confirm the number for your written scope. A per-square price only tells part of the story, though — tear-off, flashing, underlayment upgrades, deck repairs, and permits get added on top, which is why a real estimate beats any per-square guess. Quick answers

Is a roofing square the same as a square foot?

No — one roofing square equals 100 square feet. It's a bigger unit used specifically for roofs.

How many shingle bundles are in a square?

For most architectural shingles, three bundles cover one square, though it varies by product. We calculate the exact count for your roof.

Can I estimate my own square count?

Roughly, yes — measure each roof plane, multiply length by slope-height, total it, and divide by 100. For an accurate number that accounts for pitch and waste, let us measure it.

Why does my quote have more squares than I calculated?

The waste factor. Cuts at valleys, hips, and edges create scrap, so we order roughly 10% extra. That's standard and keeps the job from running short.
