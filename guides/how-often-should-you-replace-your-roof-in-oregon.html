Roof Lifespan Guide

How often should you replace your roof in Oregon?

Every 20 years? Every 30? The honest answer for a Douglas County home depends on your material, your shade, and how well the roof's been cared for. Here are realistic replacement intervals and the signs that override the calendar.

⚡ Quick answer

In Douglas County, most homeowners replace an architectural asphalt roof every 20–30 years, 3-tab asphalt every 15–20, and metal every 40–70. The Umpqua Valley's wet-dry climate tends to push asphalt toward the shorter end of those ranges — faster still on shaded, moss-prone planes. But age is only a guideline: condition decides. Widespread granule loss, curling, leaks, or a sagging deck mean it's time regardless of the number on the calendar.

"How often should I replace my roof?" sounds like it should have one clean answer. It doesn't — a roof isn't a timing belt. Two identical houses on the same Roseburg street can need roofs a decade apart depending on which one sits under a big Douglas-fir. So let's give you the useful version: realistic intervals for our climate, the local factors that move them, and the signs that matter more than any number.

If you want the deep science of roof aging, our companion guide on how long a roof lasts in Oregon goes further. This page is about the practical question of replacement cadence — how often you should actually plan for it.

Typical replacement intervals by material

Roof typeTypical Douglas County lifespanNotes for our climate
3-tab asphalt~15–20 yearsThe old economy shingle. Thin, lower wind rating; often the first to fail in valley windstorms. Many older Roseburg homes still have these.
Architectural asphalt~20–30 yearsToday's standard. Thicker, better wind and algae resistance. Expect the lower end on shaded, moss-prone roofs.
Standing-seam metal~40–70 yearsSheds moss and debris, handles our rain beautifully, and often outlives two asphalt roofs. Higher upfront cost.
Cedar shake~20–30 years (with upkeep)Struggles in constant moisture without diligent maintenance; moss is relentless. Less common on new installs here.

Notice the ranges, not fixed numbers. A "30-year shingle" is a warranty term, not a promise your roof lasts exactly 30 years in this climate. We compare the two dominant choices in detail in metal vs. asphalt for Oregon and architectural vs. 3-tab shingles.

Why our climate shortens the interval

Roofs here work harder than the national average, and the culprit is the valley's whiplash weather. From October to May the Roseburg area takes on roughly 33 inches of rain across about 130 wet days. Then July and August bake shingles at 90°F+ with almost no rain. That repeated soak-and-bake cycle makes asphalt binders brittle years faster than a steady climate would.

Then there's biology. Our shaded, damp roof planes — the north sides, and anything under the oaks and Douglas-firs that make neighborhoods like Laurelwood, Hucrest, and the wooded lots around Glide so pretty — grow moss and algae that lift shingle edges and hold water against the deck. A moss-covered roof can lose years off its life. That's why moss removal and prevention matter so much locally.

💡 Two identical roofs, very different lifespans

A sunny, well-ventilated south-facing roof in open Winston farmland may reach 28 years. The same shingle on a shaded, tree-covered lot in Glide might need replacing at 18. Location within the county — and shade in particular — changes the math more than most homeowners expect.

You can stretch the interval — a lot

Here's the good news: replacement cadence isn't fixed. Simple maintenance can add years to an asphalt roof:

  • Keep moss off. Treat it before it establishes; don't let it mat up on shaded planes.
  • Clear debris. Fir needles and oak leaves trap moisture on the roof and clog gutters that then back water up under shingle edges. Our gutter guide covers why this matters in our rain.
  • Fix flashing early. Most roofs leak at penetrations — chimneys, vents, valleys — long before the field shingles fail. Catching one bad flashing detail can prevent a decade of hidden rot.
  • Ventilate the attic. Good attic ventilation pulls moisture out and keeps the deck from cooking in summer.

What we actually see around the county

The "how often" answer looks different depending on where in Douglas County you live, because housing stock and tree cover vary:

  • Older Roseburg neighborhoods — the historic districts downtown, Mill-Pine, and pockets of Laurelwood and Hucrest — often carry the oldest roofs in the county, sometimes with layered shingles from decades of overlays done before the county's no-overlay rule. Those roofs frequently need a full tear-off replacement rather than another patch.
  • Wooded lots — Glide, the North Umpqua corridor, and shaded properties countywide. Constant fir-needle drop and shade mean moss and faster wear; these roofs tend to hit replacement at the shorter end of the range.
  • Open, sunnier parcels — much of Winston, Sutherlin, and rural Riddle. Better airflow and sun help asphalt reach the longer end of its life, provided the moss and debris are kept in check.

It's why we don't quote roof life off a chart — we look at your specific home. If you're comparing this against replacement timing, our guide on the best time of year to replace a roof in Oregon pairs naturally with this one, and the Roseburg cost guide covers the budgeting side.

The signs that override the calendar

Forget the birthday of your roof for a second — these are the tells that it's time, whatever the age:

  • Granules in the gutters and bald, shiny shingle patches — the shingles' UV armor is gone.
  • Curling, cupping, or brittle shingles that crack when touched.
  • More than one leak, or recurring stains on ceilings and in the attic.
  • Daylight through the roof boards or a spongy, sagging deck line — moisture damage underneath.
  • Widespread moss you can't stay ahead of, lifting shingle edges.

Our roof inspection checklist walks through a ground-level self-check. If you're seeing several of these, it's replacement territory — one isolated issue usually means a repair instead.

Quick answers

What's the average replacement interval here?

Roughly 20–30 years for architectural asphalt, 15–20 for old 3-tab, and 40–70 for metal — with our wet climate nudging asphalt toward the lower end, especially on shaded roofs.

Does moss really shorten it?

Yes. Established moss lifts shingle edges and traps water against the deck, and can cost years of roof life. Regular treatment and debris removal are the highest-value maintenance in this climate.

Should I go by age or condition?

Condition, always. Age is a planning guideline; granule loss, curling, leaks, and a sagging deck are what actually decide. A free inspection gives you the real answer.

Is it worth replacing before selling?

Often, in our wet market where inspectors and lenders watch roof age — but it depends on condition and timeline. We give an honest read, and the offer helps if replacement is right.
