Moss season in the Umpqua Valley: what to do now.

Every summer the moss on a shaded Douglas County roof looks harmless — green, soft, almost pretty. It isn't. Here's why our valley grows it so well, why the pressure washer in your garage is the worst possible answer, and the calm steps to take before the rain comes back.

⚡ Quick answer

Summer is the right time to deal with roof moss, because it dries the moss out and gives treatments a dry window to work. Do not pressure wash — it blasts the protective granules off your shingles and shortens the roof's life. Instead, brush loosely, treat with a proper zinc- or sulfate-based product, and keep branches and needles off the roof. If the moss is thick and coming back every winter, book a free inspection before fall so it doesn't turn into a leak.

If you live anywhere between Roseburg and Riddle, you already know the sight: the north slope of the roof goes green, tufts of moss climb the shingle edges, and by late summer the whole thing looks like it belongs in the forest. We get calls about it from Winston, Myrtle Creek, Sutherlin, Canyonville — all over the valley — and the conversation almost always starts the same way: "It's just moss, right? Can I hit it with the pressure washer this weekend?"

Please don't. Let's walk through why.

Why the Umpqua Valley grows moss like nowhere else

Moss doesn't need soil. It needs three things — moisture, shade, and something to cling to — and the Umpqua Valley hands it all three on a platter. We get well over 30 inches of rain a year here, most of it falling October through May, which keeps roofs damp for weeks at a stretch. The Coast Range and our tall Douglas firs throw long shadows across north- and east-facing slopes, so those areas rarely dry out fully even on a sunny day. And an aging asphalt shingle, once its granules start to wear thin, gives spores the rough, porous surface they love.

That's the local recipe. A roof that would stay clean for decades in a dry, sunny climate goes green here in a few winters — especially under trees. It isn't a sign you did anything wrong. It's just what our climate does.

What moss actually does to a roof

The problem isn't the green color. It's what moss does underneath. As it grows, it lifts the bottom edges of your shingles like a slow, living wedge. Water that should sheet straight off your roof instead gets held against and under the shingles, wicking toward the decking. On the north slope of an older roof, that trapped moisture is how a small moss problem becomes a rotted-sheathing problem — and then a ceiling stain in the bedroom.

Moss also holds granules that have already loosened, so it quietly accelerates the wear that let it grow in the first place. Left alone for a few seasons, thick moss can knock years off an otherwise-healthy roof. That's why we treat it as maintenance, not decoration.

Why pressure washing is the wrong fix

Here's the one thing we want every homeowner in Douglas County to remember: never pressure wash asphalt shingles. The granules on your shingles are their sunscreen and their armor — the whole reason the shingle survives our wet-then-baking-dry cycle. A pressure washer strips those granules off by the handful. You'll finish the afternoon feeling productive, looking at a "clean" roof, and you'll have taken years off its life in an hour. We've been called out to roofs that failed early for exactly this reason.

Walking a wet, mossy, Oregon roof is also genuinely dangerous — those north slopes get slick as ice. Between the fall risk and the granule damage, DIY pressure washing is the fastest way we know to turn a cheap problem into an expensive one. Our pro vs DIY moss removal guide goes deeper on where the safe line sits.

The safe way to handle moss

Done right, moss removal is gentle and patient — the opposite of blasting. The approach we use on moss removal jobs across the valley looks like this:

Brush, don't blast. Loose, dried moss can be swept off with a soft brush, always working downward with the shingle so you don't lift the tabs. No scraping, no metal tools.

Treat with the right product. A proper zinc- or sulfate-based moss treatment kills what's left and discourages regrowth, applied on a dry stretch so it has time to work before rain rinses it off. Summer's dry window is ideal for this — one more reason not to wait until October.

Let it die and fall. Good treatment doesn't rip everything off in one dramatic pass. It kills the moss so it dries, weakens, and weathers away over the following weeks, which is far kinder to the shingle surface.

Fix the conditions that grew it. Trim back the branches shading and dropping needles on the roof. Keep the gutters clear so water actually leaves. On a reroof, zinc or copper strips near the ridge release a trace of metal every rain that keeps moss from taking hold in the first place.

Keeping it off between now and winter

The homeowners who never call us in a panic are the ones who stay a season ahead. Between now and the first real rains, it's worth getting the fir needles and leaf litter off the roof and out of the gutters, cutting back anything overhanging the shingles, and giving the shaded slopes a look. When it's time to call someone up there

Some moss is just cosmetic and a light treatment handles it. But a few things mean it's worth having a set of trained eyes on the roof: moss that's clearly lifting shingle edges, thick growth that returns every single winter no matter what you do, granules collecting in the gutters alongside the moss, or any roof already past about 20 years. The short version

Moss is normal here, but it's not harmless. Handle it in summer, skip the pressure washer, treat gently, and keep the roof clear before the rain returns. When in doubt, a free inspection settles it — no guessing from the ground, no pressure to buy.
