Storm-season roof prep for Douglas County homes.

Our wind comes off the Coast Range and our rain comes in rivers. Here's how to get a roof ready before the first big system of the season — and the calm playbook for what to do the morning after one hits.

⚡ The short version

Prep is mostly about water having somewhere to go and wind having nothing to grab. Clear the gutters and valleys, cut back overhanging limbs, check the flashing and pipe boots, and lock down anything loose before the season turns. After a storm, look before you climb: scan from the ground, check the attic, photograph any damage, and call early rather than after the third leak. July is the calm window to handle all of it.

People from drier states are always a little surprised by an Umpqua Valley winter. It isn't the drama of a single hurricane — it's the relentlessness. Atmospheric rivers stack up one after another from October into spring, dumping inches at a time, and the wind that rolls over the Coast Range finds every loose shingle and tired flashing we've got. A roof doesn't have to be old to leak in that; it just has to be unprepared.

The good news is that storm prep is genuinely simple, and the middle of a dry July is the perfect time to do it — while the roof is safe to walk and there's no water in the forecast forcing your hand. Here's the checklist our family crew runs on our own homes.

Give the water somewhere to go

More storm-season roof damage in Douglas County starts in the gutters than anywhere else. When a run clogs with fir needles and moss and then a three-inch rain event hits, the water backs up under the shingle edge, over the fascia, and behind the drip edge — and now you've got rot where nobody can see it. Before the season, clear every run and every downspout, and make sure the water is actually being carried away from the foundation, not dumped against it. If you're tired of doing this two or three times a fall under the conifers, that's exactly what gutter guards are for, and our Oregon gutter guide covers sizing and slope.

While you're at it, look at the valleys — the V-shaped channels where two roof planes meet. That's where the most water on the whole roof funnels through, and a valley packed with needles and moss is a dam waiting to fail.

Give the wind nothing to grab

Wind damage is a game of leverage. Once an edge lifts, the next gust gets underneath it and peels. So before the season we look for anything already curling, cracked, or loose, and we deal with it while it's a small repair instead of a January emergency. Overhanging tree limbs are the other big one: a Douglas fir branch scraping the roof in a windstorm sands off granules and, if it comes down, can punch straight through the deck. Trim back anything touching or hanging over the roof. And walk the yard for anything the wind can turn into a projectile — loose patio furniture, an unsecured trampoline, a leaning sheet of plywood.

Check the parts that leak first

The shingle field usually isn't where storms get in — the details are. Flashing around the chimney, along sidewalls, and in the valleys does the real waterproofing work, and it's the first thing to fail. Rubber pipe boots dry out and crack with age, opening a straight path to your ceiling. Before the season, these are worth a close look; if you're not comfortable up there, it's exactly what a pre-season inspection is for. Ten minutes on the flashing now beats a bucket in the hallway in December.

Look inside, too

Grab a flashlight and spend five minutes in the attic on a dry day. Old water stains on the sheathing tell you where the roof has leaked before — and those are the spots most likely to give again under a heavy storm. Damp insulation, a musty smell, or daylight through the deck all mean the roof is already compromised and the season will make it worse. Catching that in July gives you months of options instead of a scramble in the dark.

The five-minute pre-season pass

Clear gutters and downspouts · clear the valleys · trim overhanging limbs · scan for curling, cracked, or missing shingles · eyeball the flashing and pipe boots · check the attic for old stains. Anything you're not sure about, we'll look at for free before the weather turns.

After the storm: the calm playbook

When a big system finally rolls through — and in this valley it will — resist the urge to climb up in the wind and wet to look. A roof is at its most dangerous exactly when you most want to check it. Here's the order that keeps you safe and keeps your options open.

1. Look from the ground first. Walk the perimeter. Missing or flipped shingles, a piece of flashing in the yard, granules washed into big piles at the downspouts, a limb resting on the roof — all of it is visible without a ladder.

2. Check the ceilings and attic. Fresh water stains, drips, or a sagging spot in the drywall are your early warning. If you see a bulging, water-filled bulge in a ceiling, that's the one time to act fast — our roof-leak first-hour guide walks through catching the water safely.

3. Photograph everything. Before anyone touches anything, take clear photos of the damage and the debris. If this turns into an insurance claim, that documentation is worth real money — our guide on storm-damage insurance claims in Oregon covers when a claim is worth filing and how to do it right.

4. Call early, not after the third leak. After a countywide windstorm, everybody calls at once. Getting on the list early means we can tarp and stabilize before the next system stacks up behind it. We're local — based in Riddle, serving all of Douglas County — so we're not driving in from three counties over when the roads are bad.

Know the warning signs

Not every storm leaves obvious damage, and not every bit of damage leaks right away. If you want to know exactly what to look for in the days after a blow, our guide to roof warning signs after a storm is the companion to this one. The short version: lifted or creased shingles, exposed nail heads, dented or displaced flashing, and granule loss are the tells that a roof took a hit even if it isn't dripping yet.
