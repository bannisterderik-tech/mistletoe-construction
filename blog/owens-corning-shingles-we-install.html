The Owens Corning shingles we install — and why.

We're an Owens Corning contractor, and that's a deliberate choice, not a coincidence. Here's the actual shingle we put on Douglas County homes, the technology that makes it hold in our wind and rain, the colors that flatter an Oregon house, and how the warranty really works.

The short version

Our go-to for most Douglas County homes is the Owens Corning Duration series — architectural (laminate) shingles with the SureNail nailing strip for better wind resistance. They come in colors that suit our evergreen backdrop, and because we're an Owens Corning contractor the manufacturer's warranty is a real one. But the shingle is only half the roof; how it's installed is the other half, and that's where we earn our keep.

When someone asks "what shingle do you use?" they're usually really asking two things: is it good stuff, and why that brand and not another. Both are fair. A roof is a big purchase you only make a couple of times in your life, and you should understand what's going up there. So here's the straight version — the products we reach for, the reasons behind them, and the honest caveats.

The line we install: Owens Corning Duration

For the vast majority of homes we roof around Roseburg and the wider Umpqua Valley, we install the Owens Corning Duration series. These are architectural shingles — also called laminate or dimensional — which means each shingle is built from two layers bonded together for depth and strength. That's a real step up from the old flat 3-tab shingle you still see on older roofs. If you want the full comparison, our architectural vs 3-tab guide breaks it down, but the short story is that laminate shingles look better, last meaningfully longer, and stand up to wind far better than the flat kind.

For homes that need more, the Duration family scales up. There's a heavier, more textured "designer" look for homeowners who want their roof to be a feature, and there's a more flexible, impact-tougher option for homes that take a beating from wind-thrown debris off the Coast Range. On a free inspection we'll talk through which tier makes sense for your house and budget — there's rarely a reason to overbuy, and we'll tell you if you are.

Why SureNail matters in our wind

Here's the feature that actually earns the brand its keep in a climate like ours. The Duration shingles have a woven reinforcing fabric strip laid right across the nailing zone — Owens Corning calls it SureNail. In plain terms, it gives the nails something tougher to bite into and grip, so the shingle is much harder to tear loose in a windstorm.

That's not a small thing here. We don't get hurricanes, but we absolutely get winter systems that come screaming in off the ocean, over the Coast Range, and down the Umpqua. Wind lift is one of the most common ways a roof starts to fail — a corner catches, a shingle peels, and suddenly rain has a way in. A shingle that holds its nails through those gusts is a shingle that keeps doing its one job. When we weigh materials for this valley, wind resistance is near the top of the list, and this is where Owens Corning has a genuine edge.

Colors that actually suit an Oregon home

This is the fun part, and the part people underthink. Your roof is one of the largest surfaces of your house, and you'll live with the color for twenty-five years, so it's worth getting right. A few things we've learned choosing colors for homes surrounded by Douglas fir and gray winter skies:

Deep, warm earth tones — the weathered woods, driftwoods, and warm grays — tend to look terrific against evergreens and hold up visually as light shifts through our long overcast season. Very dark charcoals and blacks look sharp and modern and hide dirt well, though they'll run a touch hotter in our short but real summer heat. And a practical local tip most people never hear: on a shaded, moss-prone roof, a mid-toned color hides the faint gray moss-streaking that eventually shows on any roof here better than a very light color does. We walk every customer through this on-site with real samples, and our shingle color guide covers the thinking in more depth.

How the warranty actually works

Warranties are where a lot of homeowners get quietly misled, so let's be plain. There are two separate things at play. The manufacturer warranty covers the shingle itself — a defect in the product. The workmanship warranty covers how it was installed — and this is the one that matters most, because the overwhelming majority of roof problems come from installation, not from a bad shingle. A great shingle nailed wrong still leaks.

Because we're an Owens Corning contractor installing their system to their spec, the manufacturer coverage on your shingles is real and intact — not a technicality that evaporates when you file a claim because the roof was installed off-spec. And we stand behind our own workmanship directly, as the local company that'll still be here in Riddle when you call. If you want to understand the fine print before you sign anything — with us or anyone — read our roof warranties guide. We'd rather you go in informed than impressed.

The shingle is half the roof

A premium shingle over cheap underlayment, skipped ice-and-water shield, and reused flashing is a cheap roof wearing a nice hat. The layers you can't see — the ice-and-water shield, the underlayment, the flashing — are where roofs actually leak. We install the whole Owens Corning system, not just the top layer, because that's what makes the warranty and the roof both real.

Why we install the whole system, not just the shingle

This is the point we care most about. Owens Corning doesn't just make shingles — they make a coordinated roofing system: the ice-and-water shield at the vulnerable edges and valleys, the underlayment across the deck, the starter strips, the hip-and-ridge caps, and the ventilation. Installed together to spec, those components are what turns a pile of good materials into a roof that genuinely keeps water out for decades. It's also what unlocks the strongest manufacturer coverage, because the system is warranted as a system.

Plenty of bids will quote you a nice-sounding shingle and then quietly cut every layer underneath it to hit a lower price. That's the roof that looks great for three years and starts leaking at the flashing in year five. When you compare estimates — ours and anyone else's — read past the shingle name to the layers beneath it. Our how to read a roofing estimate guide shows you exactly where to look.
