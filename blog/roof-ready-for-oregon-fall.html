Getting your Douglas County roof ready for fall.

Around here the rain doesn't ease in — it arrives. One dry September weekend, then an atmospheric river parked over the Umpqua Valley for three days. This is the checklist our Riddle crew runs on our own homes before that first storm, so you can beat it to the punch.

The short version

Before mid-October: clear the gutters and downspouts, knock back any moss, look over your flashing and pipe boots, sweep the fir needles off the valleys, and fix the small stuff now. A $30 tube of sealant in September beats a soaked ceiling in December. If anything looks off and you'd rather not climb up, we'll take a free look.

There's a particular kind of Douglas County homeowner who calls us in November, and we love them, but we always wish they'd called in September. The story is almost always the same: the roof was "fine all summer," the first big storm rolled through, and now there's a brown ring spreading across the guest-room ceiling. Nine times out of ten, the thing that let the water in was small and cheap to fix — a cracked pipe boot, a gutter packed solid with fir needles, a lifted shingle at a valley. All of it was visible in the dry weeks before the rain. None of it was visible from the couch.

Fall prep isn't glamorous and it isn't expensive. It's an afternoon of attention that buys you a quiet, dry winter. Here's how we work through it.

Start with the gutters — they're the whole ballgame

If you do only one thing this fall, clean your gutters. In the Umpqua Valley we don't just get leaves; we get Douglas fir needles, and they mat down into a dense felt that water can't push through. A clogged gutter overflows behind the fascia, soaks the wood, and rots the very edge of your roof — the one place a roof is hardest and most expensive to repair. Then that same water sheets down against the foundation.

Clear every run, flush the downspouts until they gush clean at the bottom, and check that water is actually leaving your foundation and not pooling next to the house. While you're at it, look at the gutters themselves: sagging sections, pulled-away spikes, and seams weeping rust are all telling you they're near the end. If you're tired of doing this two or three times every fall, this is the season a lot of folks finally ask us about gutter guards — and right now a qualifying new roof comes with free whole-home gutters, which changes that math entirely.

Deal with the moss before it drinks all winter

Moss is the local villain. Our shaded, damp, north-facing slopes grow it beautifully, and by late summer you can usually see it settling into the seams between shingles. Here's why fall is the moment: once the rain comes, that moss acts like a sponge, holding water against your shingles and under their edges for months. Wet shingles age fast and moss lifts them, and lifted shingles are exactly where December's water gets in.

Late summer and early fall — while the roof is dry — is the right time to treat it. Please don't pressure wash; it blasts the protective granules off your shingles and takes years of life with them. A gentle zinc-based treatment applied now, before the wet season, does the job without the damage. Our moss prevention guide walks through exactly what works, and if it's grown past the DIY point, our moss removal service handles it safely.

Look hard at the flashing and boots

Flashing is the metal that seals the seams where your roof meets something else — a chimney, a wall, a valley, a vent pipe. It's where roofs leak first, and it's where a fall once-over pays off the most. From a ladder or a good vantage point, look for flashing that's lifted, rusted, or cracked, and for the rubber boots around your plumbing vents. Those boots are the number-one sneaky leak in Douglas County: the rubber dries out and splits after about a decade of our sun-then-soak cycle, and the crack sits right over a pipe that runs straight down into your ceiling.

A split boot is a genuinely cheap fix if you catch it in September. Caught in January, after it's been quietly wetting the framing for weeks, it's a drywall-and-insulation job. If you want to know what good flashing actually looks like, our flashing guide lays it out.

Sweep the roof and scan the field

Get the debris off. Piles of fir needles and leaves that collect in the valleys and behind chimneys hold moisture against the roof all winter and dam up water that should be running off. A soft-bristle push and a leaf blower from a safe footing do fine — you don't need to be scrubbing.

While you're up there (or looking with binoculars from the ground, which we honestly prefer for most folks), scan the shingle field. Curling edges, bald patches where the granules are gone, cracked or missing tabs, and granules piling up at the bottom of your downspouts are all signs the surface is wearing out. One or two spots is a repair. A whole roof showing it, especially past twenty years, is the conversation our signs you need a new roof guide is built for.

Do the flashlight test in the attic

This is the test almost nobody does and everybody should. On a bright day, take a flashlight into the attic and look up. Pinpoints of daylight through the sheathing, dark water stains on the wood, damp or matted insulation, or a musty smell all mean water has already been getting past your roof. The attic tells the truth before the ceiling does, and it costs nothing but ten minutes. Good airflow up there matters too — poor ventilation traps our winter moisture and rots a roof from below.

Fix the small stuff now

Every item above is cheap in September and painful in December. A tube of roof sealant, a new pipe boot, an afternoon of gutter cleaning — that's the whole cost of a dry winter for most homes. The expensive repairs are almost always the small ones that got ignored until the rain found them.

Why fall prep matters more here than most places

People who move to the Umpqua Valley from a drier state are always a little surprised by how hard our winters are on a roof. It isn't the cold — it's the persistence. We get thirty-plus inches of rain across a hundred-plus days, the roof stays damp for weeks at a stretch, moss thrives in the shade of all these firs, and then summer bakes everything dry again. That soak-and-bake cycle is why a roof that would coast for years in Arizona needs real attention here every fall.
